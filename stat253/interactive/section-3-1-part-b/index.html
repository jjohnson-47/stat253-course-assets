Section 3.1 Part B: Counting Rules and Assigning Probabilities

Part A ended on a sample space small enough to draw. Most are not. This half is about counting a sample space you could never list, and about where the probabilities attached to its points actually come from.

Counting without listing

The multiplication rule

If an experiment happens in k stages, and stage i has ni possible outcomes, then the number of sample points is the product:

n1 × n2 × ⋯ × nk

This is the two-coin tree from Part A, generalised. Each level of the tree multiplies.

The multiplication rule on experiments you can still check by hand.
ExperimentStagesSample points
Toss two coins2 × 24
Toss three coins2 × 2 × 28
Roll two dice, recording both faces6 × 636
Roll five dice657,776

The combinations rule

The number of ways to choose n objects from N, when order does not matter and nothing is put back:

( N
n ) = N!n! (N − n)!

where n! = n × (n−1) × ⋯ × 2 × 1, and 0! = 1 by convention — there is exactly one way to choose nothing.

Order matters, or it doesn't. That single question decides which rule you need, and getting it wrong is the most common error in this chapter. Choosing a committee of three from a class: order does not matter, use combinations. Choosing a president, then a treasurer, then a secretary: order matters, and combinations will give you the wrong answer by a factor of 3!. Section 3.7 returns to this properly.

Why the formula rather than a list

Choosing 2 marines from 4 gives (4
2) = 6, and you can check that by listing: {M1,M2}, {M1,M3}, {M1,M4}, {M2,M3}, {M2,M4}, {M3,M4}. Listing is fine here.

Choosing 5 from 1,000 gives ≈ 8.25 × 1012 — over eight trillion. Nobody lists that. The formula is not a shortcut for the small cases; it is the only method that survives the real ones.

Where probabilities come from

Counting tells you how many sample points there are. It does not tell you what number to attach to each one. Whatever numbers you use, two rules are not negotiable:

The two rules every assignment must satisfy

  1. Every sample point probability is between 0 and 1: 0 ≤ pi ≤ 1
  2. They add to exactly 1 across the whole sample space: ∑ pi = 1

And the probability of an event is just the sum of the sample points inside it:

P(A) = ∑ sample points in A pi

There are three defensible ways to arrive at the numbers, and knowing which one you used matters when somebody challenges your conclusion.

1. Equally likely, by symmetry

A fair die has no reason to prefer any face, so each gets 16. This is the classical approach and it is the one that makes counting so powerful: if every sample point is equally likely, then P(A) = points in Apoints in S. The assumption is doing real work — it is exactly what a loaded die violates.

2. Relative frequency, from data

Run the experiment many times and use the proportion observed. This is where the numbers come from when symmetry does not apply, and it is the approach the study below uses.

Try this directly below Part B. The relative-frequency simulator begins with a noisy short run, keeps accumulating trials, and lets you compare fair and biased experiments against their true probabilities.

3. Subjective assessment

For one-off situations with no symmetry and no history — will this specific venture succeed? — an informed judgement, stated openly as a judgement. It still has to satisfy the two rules.

Relative frequency in practice: divorced couples

The American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy classified 100 divorced couples by the kind of relationship they maintained afterwards.

Sample space S = {PP, CC, AA, FF}. The four probabilities sum to 1.00, as they must.
TypeDescriptionProbability
Perfect Pals (PP)Joint custody, get along well0.12
Cooperative Colleagues (CC)Occasional conflict, often remarried0.38
Angry Associates (AA)Cooperate on the children only0.25
Fiery Foes (FF)Communicate through the children, hostile0.25

Take the event A = {some conflict} = {CC, AA, FF}. Sum the sample points inside it:

P(A) = 0.38 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.88

Note what these are not: they are not symmetry, and they are not a claim about any individual couple. They are the proportions observed in one study of 100 couples, which is why the sample size is worth stating every time you quote them.

Check yourself

A sample point and an event — what is the actual difference?

A sample point is an indivisible outcome. An event is any set of them.

Roll one die. The sample points are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. "Roll an even number" is an event holding three of them. Every sample point is also an event (a set of size one), but most events are not sample points.

The practical consequence: sample point probabilities must sum to 1 across S. Event probabilities do not — events can overlap, and Section 3.4 is about what to do when they do.

Someone tells you the four AAMFT probabilities are 0.12, 0.38, 0.25 and 0.30. What do you say?

That they add to 1.05, so at least one is wrong. You do not need to know anything about divorce to catch it — rule 2 is violated and that is enough.

Make this reflex. Checking that a probability distribution sums to 1 is the cheapest error-detection you have, and it will save you on the Chapter 3 Test.

Why can't you compute P(A) by counting for the AAMFT study?

Because counting only works when sample points are equally likely, and these are not. There are four types, so counting would give 34 = 0.75 for the three-point event — but the right answer is 0.88.

The gap between 0.75 and 0.88 is the whole reason the counting shortcut needs its assumption stated out loud.

Choose 5 movies from 20 to watch this month. How many ways, and does order matter?

Order does not matter — you are choosing a set, not a sequence — so it is combinations:

(20
5) = 15,504

If you were choosing five movies to watch in a particular order across five weekends, that is a different question with 15,504 × 5! = 1,860,480 answers. Same objects, different experiment, different sample space.

Carry into 3.2: you now have a sample space, a way to count it, and a way to attach probabilities to its points. Everything from here is about combining events — and that starts with two words, and and or, which turn out to mean something very precise.
